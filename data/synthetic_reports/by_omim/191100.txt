A 7-year-old male is evaluated due to seizures, skin findings, and behavioral concerns. His parents report that seizures began at age 2 with brief staring spells, later progressing to generalized tonic-clonic events. He is currently on antiepileptic medication with partial control. His developmental history includes mild global delay, with language acquisition slower than peers, and he now attends a special education program due to learning difficulties and attention problems. Parents also note behavioral issues such as hyperactivity and repetitive play patterns.

Pregnancy and birth were uncomplicated, and growth parameters at delivery were normal. Neonatal history was unremarkable. Medical history is otherwise notable for recurrent seizures and a single hospitalization for status epilepticus at age 5. Family history reveals that his mother has a history of epilepsy and multiple small cutaneous nodules on her face, though she has not pursued formal medical evaluation.

On physical examination, the child is well-nourished and cooperative but distractible. Dermatologic exam reveals several hypopigmented macules (“ash leaf spots”) on the trunk and extremities, as well as multiple facial angiofibromas scattered over the malar region. A shagreen patch with roughened texture is noted over the lumbosacral area. Cardiovascular, pulmonary, and abdominal examinations are unremarkable, with no audible murmurs or organomegaly. Neurologic examination identifies mild cognitive delay, hyperreflexia in the lower extremities, but intact cranial nerves and no gross motor weakness.

Laboratory studies, including serum electrolytes and liver function tests, are normal. Electroencephalography demonstrates multifocal epileptiform discharges, correlating with his seizure history. Brain MRI reveals multiple cortical tubers in the frontal and parietal lobes, along with subependymal nodules along the lateral ventricles. Renal ultrasound shows several bilateral hyperechoic lesions consistent with angiomyolipomas but normal overall kidney function. Echocardiography identifies a small rhabdomyoma within the left ventricle without evidence of obstruction or dysfunction. Ophthalmologic examination detects retinal hamartomas without vision impairment.

Taken together, the combination of seizures, neurodevelopmental features, dermatologic findings, and multisystem involvement has prompted referral for genetic testing to determine the underlying etiology.
