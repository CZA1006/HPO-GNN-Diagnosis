A 3-year-old male is brought to the pediatric genetics clinic for evaluation of multiple congenital contractures and delayed motor development. His parents report that since birth he has had difficulty opening his mouth, tightness in his fingers, and abnormal posturing of the hands and feet. Feeding difficulties were present in infancy due to poor suck and limited mouth opening, leading to prolonged bottle-feeding and poor weight gain during the first year of life. He has since improved with occupational and speech therapy but remains smaller than his peers.

Pregnancy was uneventful, and he was delivered at term by cesarean section because of breech presentation. Birth weight was within the 25th percentile. Family history reveals no known genetic syndromes, though his father recalls being “double-jointed” as a child.

On examination, the child has distinctive craniofacial features including a small mouth with pursed lips, giving a characteristic “whistling face” appearance, prominent nasolabial folds, and a broad forehead. The mandible is small, with limited temporomandibular joint mobility. He demonstrates severe contractures in the distal joints: ulnar deviation and camptodactyly of the fingers, adducted thumbs, and clubfoot deformities bilaterally. Elbow extension is limited, and both shoulders have mild contractures. Range of motion in distal joints is markedly restricted, with proximal joints less severely involved. Growth parameters are below average, with height and weight at the 10th percentile. Neurological exam shows normal muscle tone and reflexes, intact cranial nerves, and no evidence of weakness, but gross motor skills are delayed mainly because of joint limitations.

Diagnostic studies include plain radiographs, which show joint contractures without bony dysplasia. Echocardiography and renal ultrasound are normal. Electromyography and nerve conduction studies reveal no neuromuscular abnormalities. Laboratory tests, including CK levels and metabolic panel, are normal. A sleep study demonstrates mild obstructive sleep apnea likely related to craniofacial restrictions.

Given the characteristic facial appearance, distal arthrogryposis, and joint contractures, the clinical picture is strongly suggestive of a hereditary syndrome for which genetic confirmation is being pursued.
