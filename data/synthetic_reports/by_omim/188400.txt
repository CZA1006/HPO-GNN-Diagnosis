A 4-year-old male is brought to the clinic for evaluation of developmental delay, recurrent infections, and feeding difficulties. His parents report that he had trouble with feeding and frequent vomiting as an infant, along with poor weight gain. Developmental milestones have been delayed; he walked at age 2 and currently speaks in two-word phrases with limited vocabulary. He has also had multiple episodes of otitis media and two documented episodes of pneumonia. Parents note increased susceptibility to common colds compared to his peers.

Pregnancy was complicated by polyhydramnios, but the patient was delivered via normal vaginal delivery at term, with a birth weight slightly below average. In the neonatal period, he required prolonged hospitalization due to cyanotic episodes and hypocalcemic seizures. No prior surgical interventions have been performed besides ear tube placement for recurrent otitis media. Family history is non-contributory, with no known congenital heart disease or developmental disorders.

On physical examination, the child appears small for age with weight and height both below the 10th percentile. Dysmorphic facial features are noted: a broad nasal bridge, small mandible, hooded eyelids, and low-set ears. Cardiovascular exam reveals a harsh systolic murmur best heard along the left sternal border. Peripheral pulses are present and symmetric. Pulmonary examination is clear to auscultation bilaterally. Abdominal exam reveals no organomegaly. Neurologic assessment shows global developmental delay but no focal deficits.

Laboratory investigations demonstrate hypocalcemia with low parathyroid hormone levels. Complete blood count shows mild lymphopenia, with reduced absolute T-cell count on further immunologic work-up. Serum immunoglobulin levels are within the lower range of normal for age. Echocardiography identifies a conotruncal cardiac defect consistent with a ventricular septal defect and overriding aorta. Renal ultrasound reveals a single normal kidney without structural abnormalities. A chest X-ray shows a normal-appearing thymic shadow is absent or hypoplastic.

At this stage, given the congenital cardiac defect, immunodeficiency, endocrine findings, and dysmorphic features, genetic testing is being considered to confirm the underlying etiology.
