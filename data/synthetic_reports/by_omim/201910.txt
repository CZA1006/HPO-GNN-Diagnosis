A 6-week-old female infant is admitted for failure to thrive, vomiting, and lethargy. Parents report that she has been increasingly irritable with poor feeding and decreased weight gain since birth. Over the past two days, she has developed worsening vomiting, poor oral intake, and decreased urine output. They also noticed that her genitalia appeared atypical at birth, with the pediatrician describing ambiguous features. No diagnostic work-up has yet been completed.

She was born at term after an uncomplicated pregnancy and vaginal delivery. Birth weight was within the normal range. The neonatal course was stable until the past two weeks, when feeding intolerance and poor weight gain became more evident. Family history is significant: a maternal cousin reportedly died in infancy from an undiagnosed condition associated with dehydration. There is no history of autosomal recessive diseases previously identified in the family.

On physical examination, the infant is drowsy but arousable, with suboptimal growth parameters. Vital signs reveal hypotension (BP 65/40 mmHg) and tachycardia. Skin turgor is decreased, with dry mucous membranes. Genital examination shows an enlarged clitoris, partial fusion of the labioscrotal folds, and a single urogenital opening, giving the appearance of ambiguous genitalia. No palpable gonads are identified in the inguinal canal or labioscrotal region. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations apart from tachycardia are unremarkable. Abdominal exam reveals no hepatosplenomegaly.

Laboratory studies show hyponatremia (Na 122 mmol/L), hyperkalemia (K 6.8 mmol/L), and metabolic acidosis. Blood glucose is slightly low at 59 mg/dL. Cortisol levels are decreased, while ACTH is elevated. Serum 17-hydroxyprogesterone is markedly increased. Renal function is mildly impaired due to dehydration. Ultrasound of the pelvis identifies the presence of a uterus and ovaries, consistent with internal female genitalia.

Taken together, the clinical findings of salt-wasting crisis, ambiguous genitalia in a genetic female, and hormonal profile point toward a congenital disorder of adrenal steroidogenesis. Genetic testing is planned to confirm the biochemical diagnosis and guide long-term management.
