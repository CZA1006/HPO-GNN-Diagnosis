A 29-year-old male is referred to neurology for involuntary movements, behavioral changes, and progressive gait disturbance. His symptoms began in his early twenties with subtle clumsiness and difficulty with fine motor tasks. Over the past several years, he has developed worsening choreiform movements affecting the face, trunk, and limbs, leading to frequent dropping of objects and unsteady gait. He also reports episodes of tongue and lip biting that occur involuntarily, occasionally causing bleeding. Speech has become increasingly dysarthric, and swallowing difficulties have led to weight loss. His family has noticed personality changes, irritability, and impaired impulse control.

Pregnancy and early development were normal. He completed secondary education without significant learning difficulties and was employed as an office clerk until his symptoms worsened. Past medical history is unremarkable, with no history of major illnesses. Family history reveals that his maternal uncle experienced a similar progressive neurologic disorder with movement abnormalities and psychiatric symptoms in his thirties, but no formal diagnosis was established.

On physical examination, the patient is alert but exhibits marked choreiform and dystonic movements, especially involving the orofacial region and perioral muscles, causing frequent tongue protrusion and involuntary biting. Speech is slurred and effortful. Gait is unsteady with choreic jerks and intermittent dystonic posturing. Muscle bulk and strength are preserved, but reflexes are brisk with flexor plantar responses. There are no sensory deficits. Psychiatric evaluation demonstrates irritability, apathy in some contexts, and diminished executive function.

Laboratory investigations show normocytic anemia with mild elevation of creatine kinase. Peripheral blood smear demonstrates the presence of acanthocytes (spur cells) in significant proportion. Liver and renal function are normal, and levels of vitamin B12, folate, and copper are unremarkable. EEG shows non-specific slowing without epileptiform discharges. Brain MRI reveals caudate nucleus and putamen atrophy with associated signal changes consistent with basal ganglia involvement. Nerve conduction studies indicate a mild axonal neuropathy, while electromyography shows evidence of denervation in distal muscles.

The constellation of progressive hyperkinetic movement disorder, neuropsychiatric symptoms, acanthocytosis on peripheral smear, and basal ganglia changes supports the suspicion of an inherited neurodegenerative disorder, for which genetic testing is being pursued.
