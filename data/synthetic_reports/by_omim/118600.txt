A 45-year-old male presents with a history of recurrent episodes of acute joint pain and swelling, primarily affecting the knees and wrists. The episodes are characterized by sudden onset of severe pain, warmth, and redness in the affected joints, often accompanied by stiffness and reduced range of motion. Symptoms can last from several days to weeks and tend to recur. Between flares, the patient may be asymptomatic or experience mild, chronic joint discomfort. There is no history of trauma or infection preceding the attacks.

On physical examination, affected joints are swollen, tender, and warm to touch during acute episodes. Chronic changes include joint deformities and reduced mobility. There is no evidence of systemic inflammation or rash. The remainder of the musculoskeletal and neurological exams is unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations reveal normal serum uric acid but mild elevations of inflammatory markers during flares. Synovial fluid analysis from an affected joint shows the presence of calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate crystals with characteristic rhomboid shape and weak positive birefringence under polarized light microscopy. Radiographs demonstrate chondrocalcinosis, with linear calcifications in cartilage of affected joints. No signs of infection or rheumatoid arthritis are present. Genetic testing focusing on ANKH gene mutations has not been performed.

This presentation is consistent with a familial form of calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease characterized by acute and chronic inflammatory arthritis and cartilage calcifications, pending genetic confirmation.
