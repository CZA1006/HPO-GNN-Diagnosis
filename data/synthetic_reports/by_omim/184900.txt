A 9-year-old male is evaluated due to progressive skin tightness and limited joint mobility. His parents report that the symptoms began gradually around the age of 4, first noticed as difficulty with running and less flexibility in the ankles and knees. Over the past few years, the child has developed increasing stiffness across the arms, legs, and trunk, described by the family as the skin feeling unusually hard. Parents also note that he has an awkward gait and tires easily during physical activity. There is no history of pain, fever, rash, or Raynaud-like episodes.

The pregnancy and delivery were uncomplicated, and the child was born at term with normal growth parameters. Developmental milestones were met appropriately. Medical history is notable only for mild asthma, controlled with occasional inhaled bronchodilators. His growth has otherwise remained within normal percentiles. There is no family history of connective tissue disorders or autoimmune diseases; both parents and a younger sibling are reported healthy.

On physical examination, the child appears well-nourished and alert. Dermatologic assessment demonstrates diffuse non-pitting induration of the skin, particularly affecting the thighs, arms, buttocks, and trunk. The skin has a rock-hard feel but without erythema, edema, or telangiectasia. There are no ulcerations or pigmentary changes. Musculoskeletal exam reveals restricted range of motion in the elbows, knees, and ankles due to overlying skin tightness, with contracture tendencies in the fingers. The shoulders and hips also demonstrate limited mobility, though joint surfaces appear intact. No arthritis, joint swelling, or tenderness is detected. Facial features are tight, with decreased expressive mobility, although ocular and oral involvement is minimal. Cardiovascular, pulmonary, and abdominal examinations are unremarkable. Neurologic exam demonstrates normal strength, reflexes, and sensation but reduced agility due to mechanical restriction of movement.

Laboratory studies show normal complete blood count, renal and liver function, and negative autoimmune serologies including ANA and specific scleroderma panels. Inflammatory markers such as ESR and CRP are within normal ranges. Muscle enzymes including CK are also normal. An echocardiogram and pulmonary function testing reveal no abnormalities at this stage. MRI of the thighs demonstrates diffuse thickening of the dermis and subcutaneous connective tissue without fascia or muscle inflammation. A skin biopsy performed on the thigh shows excessive, densely packed collagen bundles in the dermis with replacement of subcutaneous fat, but without significant inflammatory infiltrates.

The constellation of progressive skin induration, joint restriction, absence of inflammation, and biopsy findings suggests a fibrotic process for which genetic evaluation is being considered to establish a definitive diagnosis.
